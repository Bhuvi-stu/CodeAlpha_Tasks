01 / Understand the threat

What is phishing?

Phishing is a dishonest attempt to trick someone into revealing information, opening a harmful file, sending money, or taking another unsafe action. It can arrive through email, text, phone calls, or even a QR code.

!

Why do attackers use it? Deceptive messages can exploit trust, urgency, or curiosity to obtain account access, personal information, or payments. A convincing message is not proof that a request is genuine.

Common forms of phishing

✉

Email phishing

A broad email claims there is an account problem and urges you to follow a link.

Example: “Your account needs attention—sign in now.”
Safer move: Open the official app or type a known address yourself.
◎

Spear phishing

A tailored message uses details about a person, team, or role to appear familiar.

Example: A message appears to come from your project lead.
Safer move: Confirm unusual requests through a separate trusted channel.
▣

Smishing (SMS)

A text message uses a delivery, billing, or account story to prompt a tap or reply.

Example: “A parcel is waiting; pay a small fee.”
Safer move: Check the carrier’s official app or website independently.
☎

Vishing (voice)

A caller pretends to be a trusted organization and asks for information or action.

Example: A caller claims to be support and asks for a sign-in code.
Safer move: Hang up and call a number from an official source.
▦

QR-code phishing

A QR code hides the destination and may lead to a deceptive sign-in page.

Example: An unexpected notice asks you to scan to “keep access.”
Safer move: Preview the destination and navigate to the service independently.

02 / Read messages carefully

Recognizing phishing emails

One clue does not prove a message is fraudulent, and polished writing does not prove it is genuine. Look at the whole request and verify it independently.

01

Sender doesn’t match

Look carefully at the full address, not just the display name. A small change can be easy to miss.

02

Urgency or threats

“Act now,” “account closed,” or threats of penalties are designed to short-circuit careful checking.

03

Unexpected attachment

An unexpected file—even one that looks routine—may be unsafe. Verify with the sender first.

04

Links that don’t add up

On a computer, hover to preview a link; on mobile, press and hold if safe to preview. Do not open it to find out.

05

Odd writing or unusual requests

Errors can be a clue, but messages may also be well written. Treat password, code, payment, or bank-detail requests with care.

TEACHING EXAMPLE — NOT A REAL EMAIL
From: “Account Support” <help@examp1e.com>
Subject: URGENT: account closes today

Hello,

We noticed unusual activity. Verify your password and one-time code immediately to prevent access being removed.

Review your account

Thank you,
Account Support

⚠

Why it’s suspicious: The address uses a lookalike spelling; the threat creates urgency; the message requests secrets; and the link destination is not shown. Never share a password or one-time code in response to a message.

03 / Check the destination

Detecting fake websites

Scammers can copy a familiar logo and layout. The address and the context of the request matter more than the page’s appearance.

FICTIONAL EXAMPLE ADDRESS
⌑ https://example.com/account

Here, example.com is the domain in this fictional teaching example.

≠
FICTIONAL LOOKALIKE — DO NOT VISIT
! https://example.com.sign-in.test/account

The actual domain here is sign-in.test. “example.com” is only a misleading subdomain.

01

Read the domain

Check spelling and the domain immediately before the first single slash after the hostname. Extra words or swapped letters can mislead.

02

HTTPS is not a guarantee

HTTPS encrypts a connection; it does not prove the site is honest or operated by the organization you expect.

03

Be cautious with sign-in prompts

An unexpected login page or request for personal data deserves a pause. Close it and open the service through a trusted bookmark or official app.

↗

Safer route: Use a bookmark you created, the official app, or an address you already know. Don’t use contact details or links supplied by the suspicious message.

04 / Notice the pressure

Social engineering tactics

Social engineering uses human feelings and relationships to influence a decision. A short pause helps you take control.

01

Urgency & fear

The pressure: A warning says you’ll lose access or face consequences.

Instead: Pause. Check through the service’s official app or a trusted contact.

02

Authority impersonation

The pressure: A supposed manager, bank, or IT worker expects immediate compliance.

Instead: Verify the person using a known number or internal directory.

03

Curiosity & tempting offers

The pressure: A surprising document, exclusive deal, or intriguing link invites a quick click.

Instead: Don’t open unexpected content; confirm what it is with the sender.

04

Prizes & rewards

The pressure: A “free” reward asks for a fee or personal details to claim it.

Instead: Don’t pay to unlock an unexpected prize; check the offer with the official organization.

05

Fake technical support

The pressure: A caller or pop-up claims your device is infected and asks for access or payment.

Instead: Close the page, don’t grant remote access, and contact support using a trusted source.

06

Familiar organizations

The pressure: A bank, delivery company, employer, or IT team is impersonated to seem routine.

Instead: Treat unexpected requests as unverified, even when the name looks familiar.

05 / Practice with scenarios

Real-world patterns, safe examples

These are fictional teaching scenarios based on widely observed phishing patterns—not claims about specific real incidents. Names, organizations, and domains are illustrative.

01DOCUMENTED PATTERN

Bank account “verification”

Scenario: A fictional bank email says access will be limited unless details are confirmed via a link.

Warning signs: Unexpected request, deadline pressure, sign-in link in the message.

Possible consequences: Account details or credentials could be exposed.

Correct response: Don’t follow the link; use the bank’s official app or known number.

Prevent: Never share passwords or verification codes from a message prompt.

02DOCUMENTED PATTERN

Parcel delivery fee

Scenario: A fictional text says a package is delayed and asks for a small redelivery payment.

Warning signs: Unexpected fee, short link, no clear order details.

Possible consequences: Payment details or personal information could be collected.

Correct response: Check tracking in the carrier’s official app or using a trusted address.

Prevent: Don’t use links in unexpected delivery texts.

03DOCUMENTED PATTERN

Password-expiry notice

Scenario: A fictional workplace message claims a Microsoft 365 password expires today and links to a sign-in form.

Warning signs: Urgency, a link supplied in the email, request to re-enter credentials.

Possible consequences: A work account could be put at risk if credentials are disclosed.

Correct response: Open the service from a trusted bookmark and report the message to IT.

Prevent: Follow your organization’s verified password-change process.

04DOCUMENTED PATTERN

Manager requests a payment

Scenario: A fictional email from a “director” asks an employee to urgently pay a new supplier.

Warning signs: Secrecy, unusual urgency, changed payment details, bypassed procedure.

Possible consequences: Money may be sent to an unintended recipient.

Correct response: Verify using a known contact method and follow payment approval steps.

Prevent: Independently confirm every unexpected payment or account change.

05DOCUMENTED PATTERN

Job offer asks for personal data

Scenario: A fictional recruiter promises a job but requests identity or bank details before a verified hiring process.

Warning signs: Unverified recruiter, premature sensitive-data request, pressure to act quickly.

Possible consequences: Personal information could be misused.

Correct response: Verify the employer through its official careers site and trusted contact details.

Prevent: Share sensitive information only through a verified, appropriate process.

06 / Make safe habits routine

Everyday best practices

Use a few repeatable habits to lower risk—at work and at home.

Verify the sender through a trusted, separate channel.
Avoid unexpected links; navigate to official sites independently.
Never share passwords or one-time verification codes.
Use multifactor authentication (MFA) and a password manager.
Keep your device, browser, and apps updated.
Report suspicious messages to your organization’s security team.
Independently verify unusual payment requests or bank-detail changes.
Confirm unexpected attachments with the sender before opening.
IF YOU ALREADY CLICKED

Act promptly. Don’t panic.

  1. Stop interacting with the page or message. Don’t enter more information.
  2. If you shared a password, change it from the official site on a trusted device; change it anywhere else it was reused.
  3. Tell your organization’s IT/security team promptly. Follow their instructions.
  4. If you shared a verification code, payment details, or sent money, contact the relevant provider using a trusted number.
  5. Report the message through your email, messaging, or workplace reporting process.

If you downloaded a file or granted access, contact IT/support promptly rather than trying to investigate it yourself.
